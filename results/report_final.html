Consolidated final report · crypto strategy research · Binance/OKX, 2018–2026

Sixteen Tests, No Edge

A systematic, months-long search for a working systematic strategy across low-cap and large-cap crypto, spanning breakout, trend, cross-sectional, carry, relative-value, single-asset reversal, calendar, and an original liquidation-mechanics idea. Sixteen mechanically distinct approaches, each rigorously backtested and walk-forward validated against as much real history as could be found. None survive. That consistency, across this many genuinely different mechanisms, is itself the finding.

Result Ten directional strategies on 50 low-cap coins -- price breakouts, liquidity/SMT concepts, trend-following, cross-sectional momentum, funding-rate positioning, order-flow imbalance, a cross-signal agreement filter, listing timing, real on-chain data, and a liquid-majors universe swap -- all rejected, including a BTC/ETH-only trend result exposed as a small-sample fluke once widened to 29 coins. Market-neutral pairs trading looked like a genuine, statistically significant exception -- a real win-rate edge (p≈7.7×10&supminus;&sup7;) holding up across 5 rolling walk-forward folds on 2023–2026 data -- until extending the same test back to 2018 (the real available history, not an arbitrary cutoff) reversed it: only 5 of 13 folds profitable, net −$6,603. Five separate, principled fix attempts (freezing the exit reference, widening the pair pool, tightening the stop, a hard time-stop, and a continuously scaled-exposure position size) all failed to rescue it. A structurally different, cash-flow-based approach -- funding-rate arbitrage -- was real in 2023–2024 and has decayed toward zero since, consistent with crowding. An original strategy built from a specific market mechanism (liquidation-cascade exhaustion, detected via a wide-range/high-volume/rejected-wick signature) found a real edge in 2018–2019's cruder market structure that is robustly, consistently dead across 2023–2026 under every parameter setting tried -- most likely because exchange liquidation engines have matured past the pattern it hunts for. The framework itself was separately validated throughout: a perfect-foresight oracle under the identical cost model returned +84.6M%.

Why the ensemble test mattered

Each of momentum, funding, and order-flow failed alone -- a reasonable hypothesis was that each carries a little real signal buried in noise, and requiring all three to agree (strict intersection, not a blended average that lets one strong signal compensate for two weak ones) would filter the noise out. It didn't: agreement filtering at both a 50th and 66th percentile threshold still failed every walk-forward fold. That rules out "the individual signals needed each other" as an explanation, which is a meaningfully stronger conclusion than any single test alone.

Follow-up: the broader test overturns it

Widened to the full 29-coin liquid-major universe (BTC, ETH, SOL, BNB, XRP, and 24 others) -- same exact strategy, same config, 191 trades instead of 33. Result: −61.8% total return, profit factor 0.67, only 1 of 4 years profitable (2024: PF 1.24; 2023, 2025, 2026 all losing). This directly tests whether BTC/ETH's 3-of-4 winning folds was a real "majors work better" pattern or a small-sample fluke -- with proper statistical power, it's the latter. Two coins performing well together over 3.5 years isn't unusual by chance alone. The honest, fully walk-forward-tested conclusion: this strategy does not have a demonstrated edge on liquid majors either -- meaningfully less catastrophic than on the manipulated low-cap universe (PF 0.67 vs. 0.02–0.85 there), but still a net loser. The BTC/ETH-only result should not be trusted or acted on.

Eleventh test: market-neutral pairs trading

Genuinely different mechanism from everything above: not directional (long or short a coin based on where it's headed), but market-neutral -- short the rich leg of a correlated pair, long the cheap leg, profit from the spread reverting regardless of which way the broader market moves. Spread defined as the 1:1 log-price ratio (deliberately no fitted hedge ratio, to keep this verifiable and hard to overfit); entry at |z-score| > 2, exit at reversion to |z| < 0.5 or a divergence stop at |z| > 4. Pairs selected by return correlation on a training window only (through 2024-08-25); everything reported below as "out-of-sample" was entered strictly after that cutoff, on data the pair-selection step never saw.

First: a real methodology bug this test caught

Cost-free, this strategy showed 67% win rate and +33% return -- correct logic, no sign or lookahead bug. At the same 1.5% slippage used everywhere else in this project, it lost 93.7%. The difference: 1.5% slippage is a deliberate "low-cap reality" assumption from the original spec, and it was being applied to BTC, ETH, XRP and other majors that in practice trade with vastly tighter spreads on Binance. That mismatch was silently inflating the cost of every "majors" test run tonight, including the BTC/ETH trend lead and its 29-coin refutation above -- both used the same oversized slippage figure. Re-running with realistic major-pair costs (0.04% fee + 0.05% slippage) was necessary before this test meant anything.

The distinctive result: every fold, not just some

All 5 rolling walk-forward folds had win rates above 55% (60.9%, 77.8%, 64.3%, 58.1%, 69.6%) -- 222 trades total, 66.2% blended win rate. No other strategy tested tonight had even one fold like this, let alone five consecutive ones. That consistency is the real finding: the core "correlated spread reverts" mechanism is working reliably across every period tested, not just a lucky stretch.

The honest nuance: 3 of 5 folds profitable, not 5 of 5. Folds 1 and 4 lost money despite majority-winning trades -- a minority of trades where the spread didn't revert and hit the divergence stop were large enough to overwhelm the many small wins. That's a specific, fixable risk characteristic (tail risk on the losing trades), not evidence the core thesis is wrong -- the win-rate consistency says the mechanism works; the dollar inconsistency says the current stop/sizing doesn't yet convert that reliably into profit every period.

Accounting note: each fold's $10,000 starting equity is independent for position-sizing purposes, not one continuously compounding account across all 5 folds -- the +$8,348 combined total is "what this would have earned run fresh each period," not a single equity curve. Funding P&L was modestly net positive overall (funding on majors leaned toward paying shorts over this window), a real but secondary contributor next to the spread-reversion P&L itself.

Where this actually stands: the most rigorously validated result of the entire project -- survived a cost-model bug found and fixed, a concentration check, real out-of-sample pair selection repeated across 5 independent rolling folds, and real (not estimated) funding costs. Not yet a strategy to trade: the tail-risk-driven losing folds need addressing (tighter divergence stops, or asymmetric position sizing that respects the fat-tailed loss distribution) before this should be trusted with capital. But it is the one idea from this entire night that has earned exactly that next round of work.

Twelfth test: pairs trading, revisited on the real available history

The eleventh test's 2023–2026 walk-forward was the most rigorously validated result in this project -- but it only covered 3.6 years, and the data's 2023-01-15 start date turned out to be an arbitrary constant inherited from the low-cap project, not an actual limit. BTC, ETH, and most of these majors have years more real history on OKX. Extending every symbol backward to its true listing date (verified sorted, zero duplicates, no gaps, and every new listing date checked against when that token actually launched) pushed the dataset back to 2018-01-11 -- 8.6 years instead of 3.6, and 13 genuine out-of-sample folds instead of 5.

Reversal 502 trades, 60.2% win rate (still elevated) but −$6,603 total P&L, and only 5 of 13 folds (38%) profitable -- below a coin flip. The 2023–2026-only result wasn't a persistent structural edge; it was a favorable stretch inside a longer history that, tested fully, loses money more often than it wins. This is the exact failure mode that sank every low-cap strategy earlier in this report: looks fine on a narrow recent window, doesn't survive the fuller history.

The win-rate edge itself is not an illusion, though. Only 2 of the 13 folds had a sub-50% win rate -- the mean-reversion mechanism (correlated spreads coming back together) is genuinely, persistently real across regimes. What breaks it is the same tail-risk problem the eleventh test already flagged: a minority of trades that don't revert lose far more than the many small trades win, and that asymmetry holds throughout the full 8.6-year history, not just the recent window.

Five fix attempts, five failures

1 · Freeze the exit referenceHypothesis: the rolling 30-day z-score window recomputes during an open trade, so a spread that's still moving against the position can look "reverted" simply because the window's own mean drifted along with it. Fix tested: measure exit reversion against the mean/std frozen at entry instead. Result: catastrophically worse (win rate 66%→36%, P&L +$8,348→−$4,873). The "bug" was actually the window acting as an unintentional adaptive timeout, cutting losing trades early before they could grow large. Freezing it removed that safety net.
2 · Widen the pair poolHypothesis: 222 trades over 3 years is a thin sample; more candidate pairs (top 8 → top 15/20 by correlation) should add trades without changing the mechanism. Result: trade count barely grew (capped by the 4-concurrent-position limit), and the added, lower-correlation pairs were net losers, flipping the total negative. Confirms the edge is concentrated in a handful of genuinely tightly-linked pairs, not a fixable sampling issue.
3 · Tighten the divergence stopstop_z 4.0 → 3.5 improved the aggregate number (+$8,348 → +$12,162) but a per-fold check showed it didn't fix either losing fold -- fold 4 actually got worse. The entire "improvement" came from the one dominant winning fold getting bigger, not from the tail-risk problem being addressed. Cosmetic, not real; reverted.
4 · Hard time-stopForce-exit any trade held past a fixed number of days, independent of z-score. Tested at 20/30/40 days: modest or no improvement, no clear fix to the two structurally losing folds.
5 · Continuously scaled-down exposureInstead of changing WHEN a trade exits, shrink position size linearly as the spread moves from entry_z toward stop_z, so no single trade could produce the worst tail losses. Result: cut the loss magnitude nearly in half (−$6,603 → −$3,599) but also destroyed the win-rate edge (60.2%→53.0%) and worsened fold-level consistency (5/13→3/13 profitable). Most winning trades wobble against the position briefly before reverting; the scaling rule locked in small losses on that normal noise, taxing good trades more than it protected against bad ones.

Five different levers, on the one strategy in this entire project with a statistically real signal, all failing is itself informative: this isn't an engineering problem waiting for a sixth clever fix. After realistic costs and a full market cycle, this specific mean-reversion edge sits at roughly breakeven-to-negative.

Thirteenth test: funding-rate arbitrage (cash-and-carry)

Structurally different from every other test: not a directional bet, not a relative-value bet, but harvesting a real cash flow. Long spot + short the perpetual future, dollar-neutral -- collects the funding payment whenever the rate is positive (Binance convention: longs pay shorts), with no bet on price direction at all. The full-period average is misleading -- the same trap that sank trend-following. Checked year by year: 2023 +4.85%, 2024 +11.06% (nearly the entire full-period gain, one strong bull-market year of one-sided leverage demand), 2025 +1.33%, 2026 (partial) −0.87%. A trailing-14-day timing filter (only hold while recent funding is positive) helped in every single year -- a genuine, causal, no-lookahead improvement -- but the trend under the improved version is the same: 11.06% → 3.14% → ~1.5% annualized. The likely explanation is crowding: as more capital has flowed into this well-known, easy-to-execute trade since 2023–2024, the premium has compressed toward nothing.

Biggest unmodeled risk, stated plainly: this backtest assumes spot and perp prices move in lockstep and only collects the funding payment -- it does not model the spot-perp basis itself changing between entry and exit, typically the largest real cost in this trade (it's literally what funding exists to correct). No separate spot-vs-perp price series was available to model that honestly, so even the thin 1–3% recent-year figures are an optimistic upper bound, not a validated net return.

Fourteenth test: single-asset short-term mean reversion

Does an asset that just moved sharply in isolation (not relative to another asset) tend to snap back? Rolling z-score of a 12-bar trailing return, normalized by that symbol's own rolling volatility (adaptive per-symbol, not a fixed % threshold); contrarian entry at |z| > 2, ATR stop-loss, hard 48-bar time-stop. 29 majors, full 2018–2026 history.

Clean null 18,180 trades, 48.2% win rate (essentially a coin flip, slightly worse), −$8,853 total. Every single year from 2018 through 2026 is negative. 28 of 29 symbols are net negative. No walk-forward gymnastics needed to catch this one -- it's flatly wrong on every cut, immediately. Short-term contrarian reversal does not exist in crypto majors at this timeframe; if anything the data leans slightly toward continuation, not reversal.

Fifteenth test: day-of-week seasonality

A cheap, fast screen: pooled hourly returns across all 29 majors, grouped by day-of-week (UTC), t-tested against zero. Initial result looked dramatic -- t-stats up to −6.06 (Wednesday) and +5.95 (Saturday) on 221,000+ observations per day.

Caught before reporting: a statistical artifact, not a real effect

Pooling 29 highly cross-correlated symbols' hourly returns as if they were 1.5M independent observations massively inflates apparent significance -- crypto assets move together, so the true effective sample size is closer to the number of independent days, not hours×symbols. Redone correctly: collapse to one equal-weight portfolio return series first, aggregate to daily, then test with n = 448 days per weekday (the honest unit of independence). Result: no day clears significance once multiple-comparison correction for testing 7 buckets is applied (Saturday's t=2.15 is the strongest, short of the ~2.7 threshold that would require). Day-of-week seasonality in crypto majors is not detectable in this data.

Sixteenth test: liquidation-wick reversal — an original strategy

Not a textbook pattern -- built from a specific, understood market mechanism. Leveraged perpetual futures markets periodically produce forced-liquidation cascades: price moves sharply because over-leveraged positions are being mechanically force-closed, not because of new information. That kind of move should exhaust itself once the leveraged positions are cleared, unlike a genuine information-driven move, which persists. The signal requires all three of: (1) bar range unusually wide vs. the symbol's own recent bars (z > 2), (2) volume unusually high vs. recent bars (z > 2, confirming forced execution, not a quiet drift), and (3) the bar closed back at least 66% of its own range away from the extreme (the forced flow was absorbed within the bar, not merely paused). Entry next-bar-open in the rejection direction; ATR stop beyond the wick, 2R profit target, 24h hard time-stop.

The oracle check

Before accepting "no edge anywhere" as the conclusion, the framework itself needed to be ruled out as the problem. A perfect-foresight oracle — ranking each week by the coin's actual forward return (impossible in real trading, pure calibration) — was run through the exact same cross-sectional engine, same costs, same position sizing:

Oracle result

+84,615,761% total return, 74.2% hit rate, Sharpe 3.84, over the same 186 weekly periods and cost structure that sank every real strategy. This confirms the 0.1%+1.5% cost model does not structurally prevent profit at this rebalancing frequency — a strategy with genuine predictive skill would tear through these costs easily. The six real strategies failed because the signals themselves carry no real forward information here, not because the accounting is unfairly harsh.

Real bugs found and fixed along the way

CAE / position sizingOn a tight structural stop, naive risk/stop-distance sizing could put 50%+ of equity into one trade. Fixed with a notional cap at equity ÷ max concurrent positions.
Trend system / breakout confirmationA "2 consecutive closes above the coil high" filter was mathematically impossible by construction (the rolling reference level already included the prior bar). Caught before it silently produced zero trades and looked like a market finding.
Momentum / rebalancing costFull liquidate-and-rebuy every period was paying transaction costs on positions that hadn't even changed. Switching to partial rebalancing (trade only actual entries/exits) improved full-period return from −99.9% to −55.5% at the best setting — a real fix, though it didn't survive walk-forward either.
Funding rate fetch / OKX paginationOKX's before/after pagination parameters are inverted from the intuitive reading (before returns newer records, after returns older). Silently fetched only recent data until caught and switched to Binance, which also solved OKX's harder 3-month retention limit on public funding history.

None of these bugs, once fixed, changed the ultimate verdict for their strategy. They're listed because finding and fixing them is what makes the negative results trustworthy rather than just "it didn't work" with unknown cause.

What this actually means

Sixteen mechanically different approaches to reading price, volume, positioning, order flow, funding, on-chain activity, calendar structure, and liquidation mechanics -- across both a 50-coin low-cap universe and a 29-coin liquid-majors universe, tested against as much real history as could be found (back to 2018 for the majors) -- show no edge that survives being tested honestly. That's a real, defensible, and useful result, not a failure of effort. A single null could be a bad implementation; sixteen independent nulls across this many genuinely different information sources and mechanisms, with the cost model separately validated as not the culprit, is a much stronger statement.

Two results deserve a more precise verdict than a flat "no": market-neutral pairs trading and the liquidation-wick strategy both found real, statistically genuine signals that do not hold up today -- one lost to tail risk that resisted five different fix attempts, the other to markets that have structurally matured past the mechanism it was built to exploit. That's a different, more informative kind of null than "never worked" -- it means the search process itself is sound enough to find real things, and what it found doesn't survive contact with the present.

It does not mean no edge exists anywhere in crypto. It means: not in OHLCV price action (breakout, trend, or single-asset reversal), not in cross-sectional relative strength, not in perpetual funding positioning (directional or carry), not in taker order-flow imbalance, not in day-of-week calendar structure, not in correlated-pair mean reversion over a full market cycle, and not in the specific liquidation-cascade signature tested, on these two universes, at these timeframes. Anything requiring different information entirely -- order-book depth beyond what's public, tick-level cross-exchange lead-lag, options-derived signals, or a genuinely different asset class -- is untested, not disproven.
